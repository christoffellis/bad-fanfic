Barnaby Quill arrived at Mudgrave Academy on a Tuesday, which everyone agreed was the worst day for arriving at anything. The gates were shaped like two enormous, disappointed eyebrows. A goose in a waistcoat checked his name against a very long list and then ate the list.
"Welcome," said the goose, in the voice of a man who had never been told no. "Dinner is at seven. Do not trust the soup."
Headmistress Plimsoll swept into the Great Pantry with her robes billowing behind her like a pair of angry curtains. She had a face like a clenched umbrella and a laugh that sounded like someone dropping a tray of spoons. "Students," she announced, "this term we will be learning absolutely nothing useful, and we will be learning it very loudly."
Barnaby sat down beside a girl named Odalys Fenwick, who was knitting a scarf for a ghost. The ghost, a gloomy accountant named Mr Treacle, kept asking her to make it longer. "It is not about the warmth," he sighed. "It is about the feeling of being measured."
The first lesson was Advanced Puddles, taught by Professor Grubb, who had not left his office since the invention of the kettle. He taught from inside a cupboard. Occasionally a hand would appear holding a piece of chalk, and then the hand would withdraw and the cupboard would cough.
"Puddles," said the cupboard, "are merely lakes with low self-esteem. Remember that, and you will never be afraid of rain again."
Odalys raised her hand. "Professor, what happens if a puddle becomes a lake?"
"Then it moves to the countryside and stops answering my letters," said the cupboard sadly.
At lunch the soup began to whisper. Barnaby remembered the goose's warning and slid his bowl very slowly across the table to a boy named Tobias Wick, who ate it without a single question and was never quite the same afterwards.
That night the corridors rearranged themselves, as they always did, because the castle was bored. Barnaby followed a trail of glowing biscuits down seventeen staircases and one slide before he found the Library of Mostly Accurate Books. The librarian, a tall woman named Miss Hartleby, was asleep standing up with a pencil behind each ear.
"You are not supposed to be here," she said without opening her eyes. "But since you are, please return this sandwich to the third shelf."
Barnaby returned the sandwich. The shelf thanked him in a small, damp voice.
In the morning there was a letter on his pillow, written in green ink and smelling faintly of toast. It said that the Moon was missing and that he, Barnaby Quill, was the only student brave enough or foolish enough to find it. It was signed with a drawing of a very smug turnip.
"This is clearly a trap," said Odalys, reading over his shoulder.
"Probably," said Barnaby. "But the turnip looked sincere."
They set out before breakfast with a map that kept changing its mind, a lantern full of nervous fireflies, and a loaf of bread that Tobias insisted was lucky. The Whispering Marsh was waiting for them at the edge of the grounds. It was exactly as unpleasant as it sounded.
A frog in a tiny hat blocked the path. "Password," it croaked.
"We don't have a password," said Barnaby.
"Wrong," said the frog. "The password was the sandwich. You gave it away. Everybody gives it away. Honestly, I do not know why we bother."
Behind them the castle rang a bell, which meant that someone had insulted the Headmistress's soup again. Mr Treacle drifted over the marsh with the end of Odalys's scarf trailing behind him like a polite comet. "I have decided to haunt this expedition," he announced. "I will be mostly useless but very emotionally supportive."
They walked for hours through fog the colour of old porridge. Tobias complained about his feet. Odalys complained about Tobias. The fireflies complained about all of them, in a high buzzing chorus that no one could translate.
At dusk they reached the Tower of Unfinished Sentences, where the walls were covered in half-written poems and the floor was made of apologies. A tired wizard named Ambrose Pennywhistle sat at the top, surrounded by spare moons. There were seven of them, and each one was slightly the wrong size.
"Ah," he said, looking up. "You've come about the Moon."
"We have," said Barnaby. "Did you take it?"
"I borrowed it," said Ambrose, with great dignity. "For a project. It was only supposed to be for a week, but then I lost the receipt."
The turnip, it turned out, had been his idea. He had sent it in a moment of weakness, hoping that someone brave would arrive and take the whole situation off his hands. Odalys put down her knitting. Tobias put down his bread. Mr Treacle began to cry, mostly out of habit.
"We will help you," said Barnaby, who was beginning to understand that nobody at Mudgrave ever finished anything alone. "But you have to promise to apologise to the sky."
"I will write it a very long letter," said Ambrose.
They carried the real Moon back across the marsh in a wheelbarrow, wrapped in three of Odalys's scarves and one of Miss Hartleby's cardigans. The frog in the tiny hat let them through without a word, though it did request a small fee in biscuits. When they reached the castle the gates had unfolded their eyebrows into a look of mild surprise.
Headmistress Plimsoll was waiting on the steps. She examined the Moon, then the students, then the wheelbarrow, with the expression of a woman who had seen everything twice. "Well," she said at last. "That is the first useful thing anyone has done this term. I am deeply upset."
That evening the soup was served as usual, and for the first time in living memory it did not whisper. Barnaby suspected it was only being polite. Across the hall the goose in the waistcoat raised a glass of something green and bubbling, and the entire school, including the ghost, the cupboard, and the sleeping librarian, raised whatever they happened to be holding.
"To the Moon," said Odalys.
"To the turnip," said Tobias.
"To Tuesdays," said Barnaby, and everyone groaned, because some things never get better.
